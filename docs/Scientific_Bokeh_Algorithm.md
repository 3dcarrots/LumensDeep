# Scientific Article: Mathematical and Algorithmic Foundations of Real-Time Bokeh (Depth of Field) Simulation via GLSL

## Abstract
This article details the architecture and mathematical foundations of implementing a realistic optical Bokeh effect (out-of-focus blur) in our software. The described system abandons classical Scatter and Gather algorithms in favor of an innovative **Screen-Space Raymarching** method with Zero-Crossing root finding. The system integrates neural network-based depth maps, physically accurate aperture simulation, optical vignetting (Cat's Eye Bokeh), chromatic aberrations, and progressive temporal accumulation.

---

## 1. Introduction and Problem Statement
Rendering high-quality bokeh in computer graphics is traditionally a complex task, as it requires simulating light scattering (Circle of Confusion, CoC) for each pixel based on its depth. In our case, an additional layer of complexity arises from the fact that the input data is a standard 2D image, and depth information is generated by AI algorithms (e.g., DepthAnything, Marigold).

The classical **Gather** method (collecting colors from neighboring pixels) suffers from color bleeding and silhouette artifacts at sharp depth discontinuities. The **Scatter** method (distributing a pixel's color to its neighbors) is extremely computationally expensive and poorly suited for parallel GPU architectures (requiring atomic operations).

To solve these problems, we developed an algorithm based on **Screen-Space Raymarching**, which allows us to find the exact light source forming the bokeh for each pixel, correctly handling depth ordering and occlusion.

---

## 2. Circle of Confusion (CoC) Modeling
For each pixel, the blur radius (Circle of Confusion) $CoC$ is calculated based on the depth map.
Let $Z$ be the linearized depth of the pixel, $Z_{focal}$ be the focal plane, and $W_{dof}$ be the focal range. The CoC displacement (in the range $[-1.0, 1.0]$) is calculated as:

$$ 
CoC(Z) = 
\begin{cases} 
\left( \frac{Z - Z_{focal} - W_{dof}}{1.0 - (Z_{focal} + W_{dof})} \right)^{\gamma_{fg}}, & \text{if } Z - Z_{focal} > W_{dof} \text{ (Foreground)} \\
-\left( \frac{-(Z - Z_{focal} + W_{dof})}{Z_{focal} - W_{dof}} \right)^{\gamma_{bg}}, & \text{if } Z - Z_{focal} < -W_{dof} \text{ (Background)} \\
0, & \text{otherwise (In Focus)}
\end{cases}
$$

The sign of the $CoC$ determines whether the object is in front of or behind the focal plane, which is critical for correct occlusion handling during raymarching.

## 3. Screen-Space Raymarching and Zero-Crossing Search
The core of the `bokeh_raymarch.glsl` shader is an iterative algorithm that finds the intersection point of a ray with the depth plane.

For each screen pixel, we cast a **single ray** in a direction determined by the stochastic aperture model (see Section 4). The ray's length equals the maximum bokeh radius ($R_{max}$).

The algorithm marches along the ray (e.g., in 48 steps) from $t = -1.0$ to $t = 1.0$, where $t$ is the parameterized step. At each step, we evaluate the discrepancy between the current position on the ray $t$ and the expected depth $CoC$ at that screen coordinate:

$$ f(t) = -CoC(Z_{sample}) - t $$

The point where an object's light reaches our pixel is the root of the equation $f(t) = 0$. Assuming scene depth is piecewise-continuous, we search for the intersection using the Bolzano-Cauchy theorem (Guaranteed Zero-Crossing method). If the function changes sign between adjacent steps ($f_{curr} \times f_{prev} \le 0$), an intersection is found:

$$ exact\_t = mix(t_{prev}, t_{curr}, \text{clamp}\left(\frac{f_{prev}}{f_{prev} - f_{curr}}, 0.0, 1.0\right)) $$

Among all found intersections, we select the one with the **maximum $Z$ value** (i.e., the object closest to the camera), as it occludes all others (Painter's Algorithm). This completely resolves edge artifacts without the need for Order-Independent Transparency sorting.

## 4. Stochastic Sampling based on the Golden Angle
Instead of a deterministic grid, we utilize a pseudo-random stochastic approach based on the Golden Angle ($\approx 137.5^\circ$ or $2.39996323$ radians). This method is often referred to as **Vogel's Spiral**.

Each pixel receives a unique pseudo-random starting angle. With each new frame ($Frame$) or accumulation iteration, the ray tracing angle $\theta$ changes according to the formula:

$$ \theta_{frame} = \theta_{random\_start} + Frame \times \text{GoldenAngle} + Noise(\text{High Frequency}) $$

The sampling radius increases proportionally to the square root of the frame number: $R = \sqrt{\frac{Frame}{MaxFrames}}$. This approach guarantees perfect Stratified Sampling of the aperture area in a minimal number of frames (typically 64).

## 5. Simulating Optical Aberrations and Lens Geometry

### 5.1. Aperture Petals (Polygonal Bokeh)
To simulate the shape of aperture blades, a trigonometric radius deformation function is used:
$$ R_{polygon} = \frac{\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{N_{petals}}\right)}{\cos\left(\theta - \frac{2\pi}{N_{petals}} \times \text{floor}\left(\frac{N_{petals} \times \theta + \pi}{2\pi}\right)\right)} $$
The ray direction vector $LensOffset$ is multiplied by $R_{polygon}$, transforming perfect circular bokeh into an $N$-sided polygon.

### 5.2. Swirly and Radial Bokeh (Petzval Lens Effect)
The swirling bokeh effect (like that of Helios 40-2 lenses) is achieved by decomposing the $LensOffset$ vector into radial and tangential components relative to the screen center:

$$ 
\vec{V}_{rad} = \vec{Dir} \times (\vec{LensOffset} \cdot \vec{Dir}) \\
\vec{V}_{tan} = \vec{LensOffset} - \vec{V}_{rad} 
$$

Scaling these components by exponential factors $\exp(-Stretch)$ and $\exp(Stretch)$ allows stretching the bokeh circularly (Swirly) or outward from the center (Zoom Burst).

### 5.3. Optical Vignetting (Cat's Eye Bokeh)
The "Cat's Eye" effect occurs at the edges of the frame due to mechanical occlusion of the light cone by the lens barrel (Optical Vignetting).
In our algorithm, if the angle between the direction to the screen edge and the $LensOffset$ vector exceeds a threshold, this sample is smoothly darkened (smoothstep occlusion), simulating the ray being blocked by the lens tube.

### 5.4. Chromatic Aberration
Transverse chromatic aberration is simulated by separately calculating final coordinates for the Red, Green, and Blue channels with different shift scales:
$$ \vec{UV}_{red} = \vec{UV}_{base} + \vec{Shift} \times (1.0 + CA) $$
$$ \vec{UV}_{blue} = \vec{UV}_{base} + \vec{Shift} \times (1.0 - CA) $$

## 6. Highlight Boost (Exponential Luma Weighting)
For physically based rendering (HDR), bright areas in the out-of-focus background should form distinct bokeh contours. Since we operate on LDR (Low Dynamic Range) images, we artificially expand the dynamic range:
During the final temporal blending of samples, the weight of each color is multiplied by a factor dependent on the pixel's brightness ($Luma$). If $Luma$ exceeds a given threshold (Highlight Threshold), the pixel's energy is amplified exponentially (Highlight Boost). This creates beautiful luminous rings (Ring Bokeh or Soap Bubble effect).

## 7. Conclusions
The implemented **Screen-Space Raymarching** algorithm with **Zero-Crossing** search is a revolutionary approach to real-time Depth of Field rendering. It mathematically resolves depth bleeding artifacts, enabling physically plausible bokeh rendering that accounts for complex lens geometry, stochastic accumulation, and spatial optical aberrations—all without the need for multi-pass scene sorting.
